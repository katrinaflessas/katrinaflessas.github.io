I did not grow up programming or spend my teenage years building computers in my bedroom. I actually came into computer science after earning a degree in psychology, serving in the military, and being out of school for a long time. Because of that, learning to code has definitely been an adjustment. Sometimes I feel like I finally understand what I am doing, and then one tiny mistake creates an error message that might as well be written in another language. Still, I really enjoy programming because I am naturally very curious and have a hard time leaving something alone until I understand how it works. Every assignment feels like a different puzzle, even if some of those puzzles make me want to close my laptop and pretend I never saw them.

## Getting Beyond “Well, It Runs”

Before studying computer science, I mostly thought software development meant writing code that works. I am quickly learning that getting a program to run is only part of it. The code also needs to be readable, maintainable, tested, and understandable to other developers. This includes using version control, documenting the program, thinking carefully about its design, and trying not to create code that only makes sense to me at two in the morning. I am still developing these skills, but I have already worked with Java, C++, TypeScript, GitHub, object-oriented programming, and data structures. My next major project will involve creating a multi-user web application with React, Next.js, and PostgreSQL as part of a team. I am excited to see how all the pieces of a real application work together, although I am sure I will also discover several new ways to accidentally break them.

## Following the Digital Trail

My long-term interests are cybersecurity, open-source intelligence, and digital forensics. Open-source intelligence, usually called OSINT, involves finding and analyzing information that is publicly available. Digital forensics focuses on collecting and examining digital evidence. Both fields appeal to the part of my brain that refuses to stop asking questions until the pieces finally make sense. My psychology degree taught me to think about human behavior, while my military experience taught me the importance of accuracy, responsibility, and following procedures. I want to combine those experiences with technical skills so I can investigate suspicious activity, identify patterns, and explain what the evidence actually shows. It is basically detective work for people who get excited about metadata, login records, and finding the one tiny detail that everyone else overlooked. The dramatic trench coat is apparently optional.

## Building Toward Something Real

I still have a lot to learn before I am ready to investigate complicated security incidents on my own. I want to become more comfortable with Python, Linux, networking, databases, web development, and common cybersecurity tools. I also want to create an independent security project, such as a program that analyzes fictional login records for suspicious behavior or an OSINT tool that organizes sources into a timeline. My current projects are still beginner projects, but they represent the foundation I need before I can build something more advanced. My goal is not to pretend that I already know everything. That plan would fall apart the second somebody asked me to center a `<div>`. I want to keep improving until I can take a complicated problem, break it into understandable steps, and build a solution that is accurate, useful, and genuinely my own.
